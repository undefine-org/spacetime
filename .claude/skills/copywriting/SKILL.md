# Summary: Harry Dry's "Learn Great Copywriting in 76 Minutes"

## Overview

This is a conversation-style talk between Harry Dry (founder of Marketing Examples) and David Perell, in which Harry teaches his core frameworks for writing effective copy. Harry argues that copywriting is the number one skill in marketing -- not a bag of psychological tricks, but the art of simple, precise communication. He walks through his three-question test for evaluating any sentence, demonstrates his full creative process for building an ad from scratch, and shares principles on writing simply, using conflict, leveraging facts, and maintaining high standards.

---

## Key Principles and Frameworks

### 1. The Three-Question Test (applied to every sentence)

Harry's central framework. Run every sentence through these three filters:

- **Can I visualize it?** -- If you close your eyes, can you see what the words describe? Concrete beats abstract. "Charging pitbull" is memorable; "better way" is not. If you can't drop it on your foot, it fades away.
- **Can I falsify it?** -- Is the statement provably true or false? When you make a falsifiable claim, you put your head on the chopping block. It makes people sit up and pay attention. Subjective adjectives ("good-looking," "intelligent") are weak; falsifiable specifics ("6 foot 2," "reads on the tube") are strong.
- **Can nobody else say this?** -- Jim Dierkey's rule: "Never write an ad a competitor can sign." If another company could swap in their logo and use the same line, it is generic and worthless.

Three "nos" = you have written rubbish. Three "yeses" = you are on to something.

### 2. The Zoom-In Test (Abstract to Concrete)

Draw a line on a page. Write "abstract" at the top and "concrete" at the bottom. Take a vague word and keep asking "What do I actually mean?" until you arrive at a concrete, visualizable object. Example: "Regain fitness" zooms in to "getting off the couch" and "running" and "5K" -- which becomes "Couch to 5K," the most popular fitness program of all time.

### 3. "Don't Talk, Only Point"

A Reddit-sourced tip for writing falsifiable copy. Imagine you cannot speak, you can only point at things. Instead of saying "gold is a great investment" (talking), point at the 50-year price chart of gold (pointing). This forces you off the adjective trail and into concrete, provable evidence.

### 4. The Three Pieces of the Copywriting Process

When sitting down to write:

- **Piece 1: Who are you talking to?** Know your audience's context. Snapchat spent $7M on a Super Bowl ad targeting an audience whose average age was 39 -- completely wrong for their product. A tourist cafe that writes "Coffee + Pastry = 5 pounds" beats the one with a blown-up 17-item menu because it understands that the tourist is barely paying attention.
- **Piece 2: Have something to say.** You need a genuine belief or vision. "Why did you start this business?" is the seed. If you are building something only for money, you will have nothing authentic to communicate.
- **Piece 3: Say it well.** This is what most people think copywriting is, but it is only the final layer. It involves rhythm, alliteration, juxtaposition, visual design, and parallelism.

### 5. The A-to-B Framework

From John Henley: "The current attitude of the consumer is the starting point; the desired attitude is the finish line. You can't start a race in the middle." Identify point A (what the customer currently thinks) and point B (what you want them to think). Your job is to string the wire between those two poles.

### 6. Conflict and the "But" Structure

Conflict is the engine of interesting writing. Harry structures stories around an implicit "but" -- without actually writing the word "but" repeatedly. Three types of conflict (ABC): A = different Approaches to the same problem, B = opposing Beliefs, C = Competitors. Even a simple before/after or problem/solution creates conflict.

### 7. Kaplan's Law of Words

"Any words that aren't working for you are working against you." Harry's corollary: "You aren't taking Kaplan's Law seriously enough." This applies not just to words but to ideas -- the strength of an idea is inversely proportional to its scope. The word "and" on a landing page is almost always a bad sign.

### 8. Facts as Foundations

Harry insists on starting with facts. A fact guarantees you are saying something. Most people open their mouths and say "wallpaper, word-shaped air, balloon smoke" -- nothing of substance. A fact is precise, grounding, and true. From facts you build stories. Example: Heinz discovered that restaurants pour generic ketchup into Heinz bottles. The resulting ad: "Even when it's not Heinz, it's Heinz."

### 9. Writing Simply = Rewriting

You cannot write simply on the first draft. You can only rewrite simply. Harry's method: write multiple versions of a paragraph, then compare them and pick the best. Show multiple versions to trusted people for better feedback. His newsletter has not contained a paragraph longer than two lines since March 2021.

### 10. One Mississippi, Two Mississippi

An instant resonance test for copy. Show someone an ad. Count: "One Mississippi, two Mississippi." If they don't get it by then, rework it. Good copy communicates instantly because consumers are never giving you their full attention.

## Notable Examples and Case Studies

- **New Balance**: "Worn by supermodels in London and dads in Ohio." Passes all three tests -- visual, falsifiable, and nobody else can say it.
- **Couch to 5K**: Zoomed in from the abstract "regain fitness" to a concrete name. Created by a Boston TV producer, not Nike or Adidas, but became the most popular fitness program ever because the name was good.
- **Volkswagen speedometer ad**: "Your car has five numbers on the speedometer. Volvo has six." Visual, true, and bespoke.
- **Corvette**: "They don't write songs about Volvos." Six words, white space, uses an enemy, and only Corvette can say it.
- **Apple iPod**: "1,000 songs in your pocket." Uses metonymy (substituting "pocket" for "media player") to be more visual, surprising, and punchy.
- **The Economist**: "I've never read The Economist. Management trainee, aged 42." No picture, no logo -- just Economist red and devastating copy.
- **De Beers diamonds**: "How can you make two months' salary last forever?" Juxtaposition of the finite (salary) and the infinite (forever). 85% of engagement rings are now diamonds because of this campaign.
- **Hinge**: "The dating app designed to be deleted." Near-perfect copy -- alliteration (D-D-D-D), juxtaposition, and no other app can say it.
- **Athletic Greens**: "You're going to need a smaller cabinet." Speaks directly to the in-crowd pain point of a cluttered vitamin shelf.
- **Tesla Cybertruck**: "Better truck than an F-150, faster than a Porsche 911." Makes an unfamiliar product understandable by comparing it to known ones.
- **Dave Kitson / The Secret Footballer**: A mediocre footballer outsold Beckham, Lampard, Gerrard, and Owen's books combined by positioning himself as "The Secret Footballer" -- pure copywriting / positioning.
- **Snickers vs. Fuse Bar**: Both taste the same, but "You're not you when you're hungry" made Snickers the best-selling bar. Fuse was discontinued in 2006. The wrapper (the copy) is what matters.
- **Loom**: Repositioned from "screen recorder" (commodity) to "async video messaging" by identifying the insight that remote communication sucks. Now worth more than the entire screen recorder category combined.
- **David Ogilvy / Rolls-Royce**: "At 60 miles an hour, the loudest noise in the new Rolls-Royce comes from the electric clock." Increased sales by 50%.

## Actionable Takeaways

1. **Apply the three-question test to every sentence.** Can I visualize it? Can I falsify it? Can nobody else say it?
2. **Zoom in on abstract words.** Keep asking "What do I actually mean?" until you land on something concrete.
3. **Don't talk, only point.** Replace adjectives with evidence: graphs, facts, comparisons, specific details.
4. **Know who you are talking to.** Picture the specific person reading this, in their specific context.
5. **Start with a fact.** Facts guarantee substance. "If in doubt, give me a fact."
6. **Use conflict.** Structure writing around opposites, before/after, problem/solution, or competitor contrasts.
7. **Write copy and design together, in the actual medium.** Never draft in isolation from how the reader will see it.
8. **Rewrite relentlessly.** Good copy takes 20-25 rewrites over multiple days.
9. **Obey Kaplan's Law ruthlessly.** Any word not working for you is working against you.
10. **Keep paragraphs to two lines or fewer.**
11. **Test with "One Mississippi, Two Mississippi."** If someone can't grasp your ad in two seconds, simplify.
12. **Maintain supremely high standards.** "Your standards are your work." Nine out of ten or it does not go out.
13. **Collect excellence.** Build a personal swipe file of great ads and writing.
14. **AI is a tool, not a replacement.** It lacks taste, conviction, and experience. "Write copy that can't be copied."
